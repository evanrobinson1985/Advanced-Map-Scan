Scan as you go

Uses My location, and the map follows you. The whole map view around you is downloaded and scanned (at least 1 km, at most 14 km² so it stays at full 1 m detail); as you near the edge of the scanned ground, the next view-sized area ahead of you is. Earlier areas' results stay on the map. Areas show green when scanned, orange while in progress. Uses mobile data: roughly 4 MB per km².

Compare scans with my caves
Your imported places count as known cave locations. After each scan (any mode, deep learning, Scan All, scan as you go), the caves inside the scanned ground are marked on the map: a green ring when a detection lies within the distance above, a red dashed ring when none does. The summary shows how many were found, by which scans, and how many detections sit at known caves. Learn from my caves measures what the Caves / sinkholes, Cave entrance, Rockshelter and Ceremonial karst scans see at each of your caves in the loaded LiDAR (within 80 ft of the point, for GPS error), keeps those as confirmed examples (similar shapes rank higher) and fits each scan's limits to take in 90% of them (the odd outlier is left out, so the limits don't open wide). Each area you learn in adds to the others: only that area's caves are measured again. With Tighten as well as loosen and 10+ caves for a scan, its limits are fitted both ways (halfway with 10–29 caves, fully with 30+), so detections smaller or shallower than nearly all your caves are cut, and detections unlike every one of your caves rank lower. The cave scans then also rank a detection higher when it sits at the heights and on the slopes your caves nearby do. Forget it takes all of that back. Which way do my caves face? measures the hillside at each cave in the loaded LiDAR and compares it with all the steep ground around them, so a lean towards the south shows only if the caves favour it more than the hills themselves do.

1. Data Pipeline — Bare-Earth LiDAR DTM

Source: USGS 3DEP bare-earth Digital Terrain Model (ground-classified LiDAR returns, forest canopy stripped) — not a canopy-top surface model. 1-meter resolution is the absolute minimum for detecting human-scale anomalies like middens or small cave vestibules.
Ready to load data.
Downloads everything in the map view, in tiles that are fetched in parallel and stitched into one seamless DTM. Auto uses full 1 m LiDAR whenever the whole view fits the memory budget, and otherwise the finest resolution that fits. The terrain is drawn with smooth bicubic interpolation, so zooming in past the data's resolution stays smooth instead of turning blocky. Finer pixels than the source LiDAR are interpolated and don't add real detail.

Method: iMound (Freeland et al. 2016). The terrain is detrended and flipped upside down, and the "pits" a fill has to raise are the mounds, measured from their own base contour, so only closed highs count, not hillside bulges or ridge noses. Each candidate is then re-tested with random 3DEP-grade LiDAR error added (stochastic depression analysis) and must persist in most runs. Finally it must correlate with an ideal conical dome or flat-topped platform (template matching, Davis et al. 2018).

Caves: Priority-Flood depression fill (closed-basin/doline detection). Mounds: Local Relief Model via morphological opening (regional-trend removal, radius set by Trend Radius above), scanned at three scales and merged since one radius alone can't see both small and large mounds well. Tap points around the base of a known mound, where it meets the natural ground, then Finish. The app fits the natural ground in a band outside your line and measures height, size, shape, sides, ground slope, elevation and borrow pits. It also records what the scanner itself sees there, which is what later scans are compared with, and tells you if your line looks tight or wide. Outline look-alikes too (tree throws, boulders, spoil piles, natural knobs): later scans rank candidates like your mounds higher, rank ones like your look-alikes lower, and skip anything inside a look-alike outline. Export saves GeoJSON (opens in GIS software); import merges, so outlines from different trips and devices combine.
Confirming a cave/sinkhole or mound — the checkbox on a marker's popup, or "This is a Cave/Sinkhole" / "This is a Mound" from click-to-inspect — only ever widens thresholds to include it and raises similar future candidates' Match Score. It never narrows the search or excludes anything unlike what's been confirmed so far. Settlement confirmation works differently, on purpose: a camp or village site can't be verified by looking at the map the way a hole in the ground can, so "Confirm Known Site" asks what actual evidence you have (artifacts, a documented record, midden staining, oral history) rather than offering a one-click checkbox. Confirmed sites boost future scans two ways: areas near a confirmed site score higher, and areas whose water/elevation/slope profile resembles a confirmed site score higher too, even far away. Each mode is learned separately. Training data lives in this browser only (localStorage) — export it to back it up or move it to another device.

6. Experimental: Deep Learning

A small neural network learns what mounds look like from your training data: confirmed mounds and mound outlines teach it what a mound is; "Not real" marks, look-alike outlines and random ground teach it what isn't. It looks at the bare-earth terrain directly (40 m squares, ground tilt removed) instead of following the scan's rules, so it may catch mounds the rules miss, or flag things they wouldn't. It needs a terrain sample of each item, which is taken whenever a DTM covering it is loaded. More mounds = better: expect rough results under ~20 confirmed mounds. Results are separate from the regular scan (purple "D" markers).
